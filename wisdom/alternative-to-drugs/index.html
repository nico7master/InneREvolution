The Alternative to Drugs

An Inne®evolution article on intoxication, consciousness, and the science of bliss

By InneREvolution·12 August 2026·13 min read

More people use drugs today than at any point in human history. According to the United Nations World Drug Report 2025, 316 million people worldwide now use drugs — over 6% of the global population aged 15 to 64. The cost of failing to treat drug use disorders is steep: nearly half a million deaths and 28 million healthy years of life lost to disability and premature death in a single year. And of all the people struggling with drug use disorders, only one in twelve receives any form of treatment.

Behind every one of those numbers is a human being looking for the same thing: a little relief, a little peace, a little joy. A moment where the mind stops torturing them.

We tend to talk about this as a moral failure, a criminal problem, or a disease — but what if the framing itself is wrong? What if the drug pandemic is not really about drugs at all?

It Was Never About the Drug

Sadhguru has spent decades addressing young people about substance use, and his starting point is unusual: he refuses to treat it as a moral issue.

I am not someone who looks at drugs as a moral issue. It is a tragedy — a tragedy of destroying the immense possibilities of the human mechanism.

In his teaching, the urge to drink, smoke, or swallow is not hedonism. It is a misplaced spiritual longing — the natural human hunger for intensity, expansion, and boundlessness, pointed at the wrong door. Every human being seeks to enhance their life. The tragedy is that for many people, the deepest experience they have ever known is physical or emotional pain. Their moments of peace and joy are fragile and superficial — so when they hear rumors of a deeper dimension of life, they reach for the fastest available chemical route to it.

The question Sadhguru asks is simpler and more radical: why does anyone want to escape their own experience of life in the first place?

His answer: the unconsciously operated mind is a misery-manufacturing machine. People do not become dependent on a substance because it is wonderful — they become dependent because for a few hours it switches off what he bluntly calls the torture of the mind. The relaxation, the high, the relief — none of it comes from the chemical itself. It comes from the temporary absence of psychological suffering.

This is why the epidemic keeps growing. Alcohol was the previous generation's answer. This generation, not wanting to repeat their parents, is simply "upping the dose" — Sadhguru calls it a natural process of graduation. Same urge, bigger chemical. The UN numbers confirm it: global drug use has risen roughly a third in a decade, driven increasingly by synthetic drugs like methamphetamine.

And the demand side keeps deepening. During the COVID-19 pandemic, anxiety and depression increased two- to three-fold — by December 2020, 42% of surveyed U.S. adults reported symptoms of anxiety or depression, against 11% before. Sadhguru's observation of affluent societies is even starker: about one-fifth of the population is on regular psychiatric medication just to stay mentally balanced. "Comfort has come like never before," he says, "but well-being has not come."

A population that cannot bear to be with itself will find something to numb itself. That is not a character flaw. It is a chemistry problem — and chemistry problems have chemistry solutions.

What the Chemical Actually Does

The yogic tradition did not wait for modern pharmacology to study intoxicants; it examined them with the same precision it brought to breath and mind.

Sadhguru describes drugs like LSD and marijuana as literally mind-blowing: they crack the psychological structure so that for a moment, a person can look at existence through the crack, without the filter of the mind. It feels fantastic — Patanjali, the father of yoga, himself acknowledged that a drug is a possibility to divinity.

But it is, he says, the lowest possibility — and a brutal one. A few things happen when you take the chemical route:

  • The trap tightens. When the drug wears off, you return to the boundaries of body and mind feeling more suffocating than ever before. To recreate the experience, the dosage must keep rising — until the trips stop coming, and what remains is a helpless, retarding dependence. The dosage grows; the human being shrinks.

  • Discrimination is pulled down. Substances artificially suppress the discretionary mind. Whatever is in you gets exaggerated: if you are joyful, you feel more joyful; but if there is fear or unpleasantness underneath, it can explode into terrifying fear-trips and hallucination.

  • Perversion is activated. In classical yoga, alcohol specifically stimulates viparyaya — the faculty of wrong knowledge, the brain's center of perversion. Chemically triggered, it makes the whole world look perverted, agitates the body and mind, and moves the system in exactly the opposite direction of meditative stillness.

  • The odds are terrible. Of those who use chemicals as a route to transcendence, Sadhguru says, generally only one in a hundred comes out free. The other ninety-nine come out with a drug problem.

And what about the legends of Shiva smoking hash? Sadhguru's answer has become a one-line teaching: "Shiva can contain it, not you." Lore says Adiyogi did not take substances to get high — his energies were so refined and vast that he partook of grosser elements to bring inertia into his system, just to stay rooted in the physical body. For him it was a downward trip. For you it would simply retard your growth. The story of Adi Shankara makes the same point with molten iron: when disciples copied their master's drinking, Shankara walked into a blacksmith's shop and drank the molten metal — then invited them to copy that too.

The Most Sophisticated Chemical Factory on the Planet

Here is where the story turns — and where ancient yogic insight and modern science meet almost word for word.

An Israeli organic chemist spent nearly thirty years studying the brain and discovered something astonishing: the human brain is packed with millions of cannabis receptors. A receptor exists to receive a chemical. Why would your brain have receptors for cannabis unless it expected a cannabis-like molecule of its own?

It does. The body manufactures its own bliss molecule — and when the researcher searched the world's scriptures for a name, only the Indian tradition had a word for the thing itself: ananda, bliss. The molecule was named anandamide.

Sadhguru's comment on this has become one of his signature teachings:

This human mechanism is the most sophisticated chemical factory on the planet. The question is only: are you a great CEO or a lousy CEO?

He speaks of a "whole marijuana garden" — a whole mountain — inside every human being: the capacity to be stoned and blissful by one's own chemistry, all the time, with the mind left fully sharp and alert.

The profound joke of the drug pandemic is that people are buying, smuggling, overdosing, and dying for a diluted, external copy of a chemical their own body can manufacture. They are paying dealers for what their own system would produce for free — if only someone had taught them how to run the factory.

What the Science Shows

This is no longer just a metaphor. A research group at Harvard Medical School–affiliated Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center has been measuring what happens when you activate that internal factory through Isha Yoga practices.

The anandamide study. In a prospective study published in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine (2020), researchers measured blood biomarkers in participants of a 4-day Isha Yoga retreat. After the retreat, all major endocannabinoids — including anandamide — increased by more than 70% (P < 0.001), along with a nearly three-fold increase in BDNF, a protein supporting brain health and resilience. Depression scores dropped 26%, anxiety dropped 23%, and measured happiness and psychological well-being rose significantly. The improvements were still present one month later.

Let that sink in. A few days of yogic practice raised the body's own bliss chemistry — the exact chemistry people risk prison and death to ingest — with no dosage, no dealer, no damage, and no hangover.

Isha Kriya for anxiety and depression. A study published in Frontiers in Psychiatry (2022) examined a freely available 12–18 minute guided meditation, Isha Kriya, practiced online. Within two weeks, mean anxiety scores fell from 25.4 to 16.8 (a large effect size of 1.31), and depression scores fell from 15 to 8.81 — improvements sustained at four and six weeks.

Inner Engineering and stress. Research published in Frontiers in Psychology (2022) on the Inner Engineering Completion Online program — whose core is a 21-minute practice called Shambhavi Mahamudra Kriya — found perceived stress scores dropped significantly immediately after the program, and fell a further 3.5 units after six weeks of daily practice, alongside improvements in sleep quality.

Well-being and work. A study in the Journal of Integrative and Complementary Medicine (2022) with 347 participants found the Inner Engineering Online program measurably increased mindfulness, joy, vitality, restfulness, and a felt sense of oneness — along with meaningful work, psychological capital, and work engagement.

Four peer-reviewed studies. Different measures, different populations, one pattern: when people learn to manage their inner chemistry consciously, the symptoms they were self-medicating begin to dissolve.

Below the Mind, or Beyond It

Sadhguru draws a clean line between the two paths human beings take when the mind becomes unbearable:

  • Drugs take you below the mind. They dull or crack the instrument so the torture temporarily stops. You descend below consciousness, below discrimination — you borrow a brief holiday from being human, and return with interest due.

  • Meditation takes you beyond the mind. Not by damaging the instrument, but by creating a distance between you and it. The mind is still there — thoughts still arise — but you are no longer trapped inside it. You become the one who has a mind, rather than the one who is the mind.

One path is borrowed, brief, and billed later — in health, in clarity, in dependence. The other is self-generated, sustainable, and compounds over time.

And here is the surprise: yoga is not anti-intoxication. Sadhguru says he is one hundred percent for intoxication — the yogis are simply too greedy for the small stuff:

I have never touched a substance in my life, but if you look at my eyes, I am always stoned. We look at alcohol and drugs as kindergarten stuff, because we can get intoxicated a thousand times over just with our aliveness. Why simply wine? You can get drunk with the di-vine!

The goal of yoga is to be totally drunk and hundred percent alert at the same time — stoned and stable, as the lore of Adiyogi puts it, whose crescent moon symbolizes somarasa, the perennial inner nectar. External chemicals can never give you that combination: awareness and ecstasy, together. Your own chemistry can.

Why "Just Say No" Never Worked

If addiction were a moral failure, punishment would cure it. If it were simple ignorance, education would cure it. Fifty years of the war on drugs suggests neither is true.

Sadhguru's insight explains why: you cannot counsel a person out of a state you cannot give them. Someone on chemicals feels artificially enhanced; words bounce off. And you cannot tell a person in desperate pursuit of happiness to simply stop — they will find devious ways to continue.

The only way to make someone drop the small experience is to give them a bigger one. Once a person learns to become ecstatic by their own chemistry, the need for substances falls away by itself.

This is the real argument for yoga — not prohibition, not discipline, not fear. Yoga is not asking anyone to give up joy. It is asking them to stop settling for a cheaper, borrowed version of it. Nobody has to be convinced to abandon a small pleasure once they have touched a bigger one. When simply sitting and breathing becomes an enormous pleasure, dependencies do not have to be fought — they fall away by themselves.

Even the "dosage" logic of yoga mirrors and inverts the logic of addiction: a small daily practice produces gradual change; a fuller immersion produces transformation. The difference is that increasing the dose of yoga builds you up rather than breaking you down.

The Deeper Root: An Unmanaged Interiority

The drug pandemic is one symptom of a larger disease: a civilization that has organized the outside brilliantly and the inside not at all.

For 150 years, humanity has worked furiously to fix the external environment — and succeeded. But the inside was left unmanaged, and the statistics of the inner world went the other way. Sadhguru points out that most people are suffering not the Creator's creation but their own "petty creation":

The planet is spinning on time — not a small event. All galaxies are going perfectly well, the whole cosmos is doing great. But you have one nasty little thought crawling through your head, and it is a bad day.

He even recounts the note left by a media anchor who took her own life: "My brain is my enemy." Millions are suffering their own evolved cerebral capability simply because nobody taught them how to handle it. Add to this a sedentary life that leaves physical energy unspent and turning inward as anxiety, a culture where over ninety percent of people suppress their natural emotions, and a social architecture of ever-tighter isolation — "exclusiveness," Sadhguru notes, "naturally leads to depression" — and you have the perfect demand curve for the global drug supply.

In the classical yogic framework, this is precisely backwards from how we live. Well-being is not a prize to win at the end of life; it is not even a goal. Peace and happiness are Square One — the foundation on which everything else, body and intellect included, is built. A human being who is pleasant within does not need to escape. A human being who is ecstatic by their own chemistry does not need a dealer.

That is why Sadhguru insists inner wellbeing is not a luxury or a hobby for this generation — it is the missing piece without which every other fix fails.

Where to Begin

You do not need a Himalayan cave, an ashram, or years of preparation. The entry points used in the studies above are simple and accessible:

  • Isha Kriya — a free, guided 12–18 minute meditation available online. In a clinical study it measurably reduced anxiety and depression within two weeks of daily practice.
  • Shambhavi Mahamudra Kriya — a 21-minute practice learned through the Inner Engineering program, shown to reduce stress and improve sleep with regular practice.

Sit. Breathe. Practice a little every day. In the beginning it seems too simple to matter — until you notice that the moments of peace are no longer something you have to purchase, swallow, or smoke. You have started doing what no dealer can do for you: turning on your own pharmacy.

The End of the Search

The drug pandemic is, at its root, a pandemic of people who have not yet found access to their own inner nature. Three hundred million people, looking outside for something that can only be produced inside.

Yoga does not fight drugs. It makes them irrelevant. It replaces escape with access, numbness with clarity, borrowed highs with a bliss that belongs to you — one that does not fade, does not bankrupt you, and does not demand an ever-larger dose.

Adiyogi's promise, fifteen thousand years old and now measured in blood samples, is still the most radical health intervention available to a human being:

You can be stoned and stable. Drunk and aware. Ecstatic by your own chemistry.

The mountain was inside you all along.
