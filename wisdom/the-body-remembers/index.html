The Body Remembers: Why Yoga Works Where Talking Stops

A classical hatha yoga perspective on somatic memory, and why what lives in the body cannot always be reached from the mind.

By InneREvolution·2 September 2026·11 min read

There is a finding from trauma research that should have changed psychology forever, and for the most part, it didn't change the practice of it.

In 1994, the Harvard psychiatrist Bessel van der Kolk published a paper with a title that became a movement: The Body Keeps the Score. His argument, now supported by decades of neuroscience, is that overwhelming experiences are not stored the way stories are stored. They are encoded in the body itself — in movement patterns, muscle tension, hormonal set-points, and the reactivity of the nervous system. This is somatic memory: a body-based record that operates independently of your narrative memory. The body can respond to a trigger with full physiological alarm even when the conscious mind remembers nothing at all.

That single finding quietly dismantles the assumption underneath most of modern mental health care: that if you can talk about a problem, understand it, and reframe it, you have treated it. For a significant share of suffering, the conversation is happening in the wrong room. The problem does not live in the story. It lives in the tissue.

Classical yoga said this thousands of years earlier — with more precision, and with a technology to do something about it.

A Billion Times More Memory

We tend to think memory is what the mind does. Sadhguru turns that assumption upside down.

You may not remember consciously what happened twenty-five years ago, but it is working on you. What happened twenty-five hundred years ago is still imprinted on your body. What happened twenty-five million years ago is still encrypted in your body. Your mind might have forgotten life as a unicellular being, but your body still remembers. Your mind might have forgotten your great-grandmother, but her nose still sits on your face. People think memory means only mind. But that is not the case. The volume of memory that the body carries is a billion times more than the volume of memory your mind is capable of carrying." — Karma: A Yogi's Guide to Crafting Your Destiny

This is not mysticism dressed up as biology. It is biology dressed up as biology — genetics itself, as he points out, is memory. Your DNA remembers the kind of nose your great-grandfather had and puts it on your face. Every cell in your body is a historical archive: evolutionary memory, genetic memory, elemental memory, layered on top of everything you have personally lived through.

The yogic science gives this physical record a name: runanubandha — the physical memory the body carries of every deep contact it has had. Blood relationships, intimate relationships, even the sustained impressions of daily life — the body keeps a detailed, non-judgmental record of all of it. Not because it is sentimental. Because that is what a body is: memory, rendered into flesh.

What Goes Unexpressed Does Not Evaporate

Here is where the teaching becomes uncomfortably concrete. In The Path of Meditation, Sadhguru walks through an ordinary office scene:

Imagine you are at work and your boss says something to you and you become angry, but you cannot tighten your fists. What will happen to all this energy which has just been created in you? Energy is never destroyed. This energy will cripple a part of my body. It will be used to create a blockage in some part of my body.

Suppressed anger does not dissipate. Suppressed grief does not dissolve because you never spoke it. The energy of an unexpressed experience has to go somewhere — and in the yogic understanding, it goes inward, settling into the system as chronic tension, structural distortion, illness, or compulsion.

Modern somatic research has arrived at the same territory from the other direction. Clinicians studying trauma describe how unprocessed experience is encoded in body-based procedural memory — muscle bracing, postural guarding, physiological arousal — that persists for decades and can fire at full intensity without any conscious recollection of the original event. Different vocabulary, same observation: the body stores what the mind refuses or fails to process.

You may even have noticed this in your own body, as Sadhguru observes in Karma: you may exercise as much as you like, yet one muscle is invariably less cooperative than the others. Check it with a doctor, run an MRI — the report says everything is fine. The body's deeper record is not visible to an MRI. It is written in the geometry of the system itself.

Why Talking Often Doesn't Reach It

Now we can address the uncomfortable question directly. If much of what troubles people is stored in the body — in chemistry, structure, and energy — what exactly is talk therapy doing about it?

Let's be fair first, because the record deserves it. Psychotherapy demonstrably helps many people, and in acute crisis it can be what keeps someone alive and functional. Sadhguru does not dismiss it: therapies are for ill people, he says, and they can stabilize a broken mind enough for a person to function — even, eventually, to come to yoga. The critique is not that talking has no value. The critique is about what talking can and cannot do.

What the data shows. The outcomes of talk-based mental health care are more modest than the public image suggests. In a large meta-analysis of psychotherapies for depression, roughly one third of patients remitted after therapy — compared to 7–13% in control conditions. Of those who do recover, more than half are likely to relapse within several years. One systematic review estimated the additional value of psychotherapy over and above other care at around 14%. Real, but partial. Partial in a way that makes sense if the treatment is operating on one level of a multi-layered system.

What Sadhguru says. His critique is sharper than any statistic, and characteristically unsentimental:

Modern psychoanalysis has done phenomenal damage to the spiritual possibilities of what a human being could be — because of analyzing all the rubbish and giving it meanings. All your thought and emotion is just a recycling of what you have gathered." — Bha-ra-ta: The Rhythm of a Nation

From the yogic view, thought and emotion are not original creations; they are recycled data from the past. Years spent analyzing them is years spent excavating the storage room instead of cleaning out the house. He is also bluntly funny about the method itself: "Most people cannot be talked out of anything; they will talk you out of it." Anyone who has watched a clever mind defend its own patterns will recognize the truth of that.

And there is a structural observation that lands harder than any joke: psychology and psychiatry were built almost entirely on the study of sick people. As he puts it, Freud never found a Buddha to study. The field's baseline of "health" — the best outcome it can imagine — is a person who functions: "From unmanageable madness, they can bring you down to manageable madness." Adjustment to a disturbed society is the ceiling of the approach, not the end of human possibility.

The Mind Lies. The Body Does Not.

Why does a purely mental approach keep hitting a wall? Sadhguru's answer is one of the most useful sentences in all his teaching:

What happens in the mind is never trustworthy. The mind can deceive you in a million different ways. But what happens in the body is real. Your mind has lied to you a million times. Has your body ever lied to you?" — Enlightenment: What It Is

Every therapy client has watched this happen: the insight is understood, the reframing is done, the new story is polished — and two weeks later the same anger, the same compulsion, the same collapse. Sadhguru explains why in Karma:

You may decide with great determination to abstain from certain substances or to wake up early every morning. You may even manage for a while, but that is only in the initial phase. Mental determination alone cannot eradicate physical memory.

The karmic record, he says, is not stored as an argument the mind can lose. It is stored as chemical deposits and structural grooves in the system itself. You cannot out-argue chemistry. You can only change chemistry.

There is also a subtler warning here, one that practitioners of long-term analysis know in their bones: attending to a problem too closely can feed it. "Your madness also, you attend to it too much," he advises in Mystics' Musings. Like constantly poking an injured leg, relentless excavation of one's own wounds can keep them inflamed. Some of what talk therapy calls progress, the body experiences as rehearsal.

Working From the Other End

If talk works top-down — mind hoping to influence body — yoga works bottom-up: body, breath, and energy transforming the mind from below. This is not a replacement claim; it is a different instrument for a different layer.

The research on body-based approaches to trauma increasingly supports this logic. In a randomized controlled trial, sixty-four women with chronic, treatment-resistant PTSD — people talk therapy had already failed — were assigned to trauma-informed yoga or to supportive health education. The yoga group showed significantly greater reductions in PTSD symptoms, with many participants no longer meeting criteria for the diagnosis by the study's end. Trials of breathing-based practices have independently shown improved emotion regulation and heart-rate variability — the physiological signature of a nervous system that has come out of siege.

This is exactly what the classical framework predicts. If the record is stored physiologically, the practice must operate physiologically:

Human conditioning runs deep on the levels of body, mind, and energy. Merely addressing karma on one level will not work beyond a point. The swiftest and most effective path to joy and freedom is to address karma on all three levels — the physical, the psychological, and the energetic." — Karma: A Yogi's Guide to Crafting Your Destiny

Asanas are not stretches with a spiritual logo. They are a deliberate restructuring of physical memory — loosening the grooves the system has settled into, one held posture at a time. Breath is the lever that changes chemistry on demand. Together, they reach the layers that conversation structurally cannot.

Sadhguru keeps the two approaches in honest proportion with a single line:

Therapies can help you to come to yoga, but yoga is not a therapy. Yoga is for a higher order of health, a different order of health — a different type of being and wholeness. Therapy can, at the most, make you adjusted." — Yoga: Understanding the Mechanics of Life

That sentence is worth sitting with. The question was never whether therapy is useless. The question is whether adjusted is the word you want at the end of your life's healing story.

Where to Begin

If the body keeps the record, the body is also where you start reading it:

  • Your daily practice, in the body, at the same hour — if you have not yet learned a practice, that is the first step: learn one properly, from a teacher, as it was designed. If you have, the change happens in the doing, not in understanding the doing. The body does not learn from lectures; it learns from repetition, alignment, and attention.
  • Attention turned inward, into the body — a few minutes a day, simply feeling: where is there holding, bracing, numbness? Not to fix anything. The body has been waiting years for someone to just notice it. Being noticed is where release begins.
  • Let the practice work below the story — you do not need to know why your shoulders are armored. You do not need a narrative for every knot. The classical approach trusts the practice to work on layers the analytical mind cannot even locate.

One small experiment for this week: before your daily practice, sit for one minute and simply feel the body from the inside — no analysis, no story, just sensation. Whatever you find there, that is the actual archive. The mind will offer commentary. You do not have to buy it.

The truth is, your body has been trying to talk to you for years — through tightness, through fatigue, through the illness that always arrives in the same season of life. It never learned your language. It never needed to. It has been speaking the only honest language there is, and the first act of yoga is simply this: to begin listening from the inside.

The mind will tell your story. The body is the story. And unlike the mind, the body is willing — patiently, structurally, one posture at a time — to let it go.
