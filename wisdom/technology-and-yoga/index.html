Technology and Yoga

An Inne®evolution article on technology, artificial intelligence, and the oldest human upgrade

By InneREvolution·19 August 2026·12 min read

Right now, somewhere on Earth, a phone is lighting up. It happens roughly 96 times a day for the average American — once every ten minutes of waking life. Globally, we spend about six hours and forty minutes on screens every single day. That is 47 hours a week — more than a full-time job — spent looking at glass.

Meanwhile, another revolution accelerates in the background: machines that write, reason, diagnose, and create. Half of American workers say they are worried about what AI will do to their future; early-career workers report rising anxiety about whether there will be anything left for them to do at all.

We have never been this connected. And we have never been this scattered.

Sadhguru has been talking about this moment for decades — not with technophobia, but with something far more unsettling: precision. His verdict on technology can be summed up in one image he returns to again and again:

If you were a small little ant, you could have walked on anybody. There wouldn't be any problem. But if you become an elephant, now you must be very careful where you put your foot. As humanity, we have become real giants because of technology. We are hugely empowered. Now, how we walk is very important. Otherwise we will uproot everything.

This article is about where we put our foot next.

The Jingoism of Gadgets

Sadhguru coined a phrase for our cultural condition: the jingoism of gadgets — a proud, almost patriotic devotion to devices that quietly erodes the one faculty they all depend on: human attention.

Parents proudly note that their children don't have attention, but like Facebook. If human attention fails, all your gadgets will fail you one day. These devices are small external extensions of the human system. If the primary 'I' fails, no iPhone will save you.

Every device you own is a downstream product of attention. Someone had to pay attention long enough to design it, code it, build it. The devices do not generate attention; they consume it. And the science confirms what Sadhguru observes:

  • In a landmark experiment by Ward et al. (Journal of the Association for Consumer Research), the mere presence of your own smartphone — off, face-down on the desk — measurably reduces available cognitive capacity. The brain spends effort not checking it. Researchers call it brain drain.

  • A 2025 study in PNAS Nexus found that simply blocking mobile internet for two weeks improved participants' mental health "more than antidepressants" and restored sustained attention "as much as being 10 years younger."

Read that second finding twice. Two weeks away from the feed produced changes bigger than medication. The problem was never the hardware. It was the relationship.

Far Have Become Near, Near Have Become Far

Perhaps the strangest achievement of the digital age is that we solved distance while breaking proximity.

Those who are far have become near, those who are near have become far because of electronic communication. People are in your house, but they are watching the television. Are they with you? They are somewhere else.

The average person spends over two hours a day on social media — what Sadhguru calls "gossip gone global." Families sit in the same room, each alone. We carry every voice in the world in our pocket except the ones standing next to us. Loneliness has become a public health crisis in the most connected era of human history — not despite the technology, but because of how compulsively we use it.

And there is a deeper cost, one Sadhguru frames with characteristic bluntness: we have moved from intuition to information. Children trust Google over their parents; we confuse the accumulation of data with knowing. But data is dead knowledge. Intuition — perception without memory — was always the living edge of human intelligence, and we are training ourselves out of it, one search query at a time.

The Most Sophisticated Device Was Never for Sale

Here is where Sadhguru's critique turns from diagnosis to comedy. He tells a story:

Let's say you know nothing about computers and I gave you an Apple Air... Very thin and sharp. You took it home and started chopping cucumbers. It works very well. But isn't it a tragedy that you are using a computer to chop cucumber? Nothing wrong with the cucumber, but something definitely wrong with you, isn't it?

His point: surveys show people use only seven percent of even a dumb phone's capabilities. Most humans employ well under one percent of their own system's potential. The most advanced piece of technology you will ever own — a self-updating, self-repairing, consciousness-bearing biological computer — is being used primarily for survival logistics and scrolling.

Every machine humans have ever built is a clumsy copy of something the body already does. Cameras copied eyes. Radar copied echolocation. Neural networks copy brains — badly. The original remains superior, and it came free with your birth.

What Machines Can Never Do: Perception

So what happens when AI gets genuinely good? Sadhguru's answer surprises people who expect either doom or denial.

He is optimistic about AI. Within fifteen to twenty years, he predicts, pure information will be worthless — instantly available everywhere. Those whose value lies only in carrying information will find it dissolved. Good riddance, he'd argue: this forces humanity toward a dimension machines cannot touch.

A machine can accumulate data, analyze patterns, and execute actions. What it can never do is perceive — contact reality directly, without memory mediating the experience. In the yogic framework, this capacity has a name: chitta — intelligence unsullied by memory, the deepest dimension of mind that connects you to the basis of creation itself.

AI, in this view, is not humanity's replacement. It is humanity's mirror. By making information free, it makes the question unavoidable: if knowing facts is worthless, what exactly is a human being for? Sadhguru's answer: when machines take over menial tasks, we finally get what he calls a "permanent holiday" — liberation into the aesthetics and depths of life that no algorithm can compute.

The catch is that we must evolve fast enough to use that freedom. And here is the stark biological fact behind his teaching: the physical phase of human evolution is complete. The brain cannot grow larger or denser. From here, there is only one direction of evolution left:

Once you come in the human form, you can only evolve consciously. Unconscious evolution is over for you. The evolutionary process is in your hands now.

Technology made us giants. Only consciousness can keep the feet gentle.

What the Science Says: Classical Hatha Yoga Trains Attention

If attention is the faculty everything else depends on, then the critical question becomes empirical: can it be trained by the body itself?

The research on classical hatha yoga says yes:

  • In a randomized controlled trial (Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine, 2017), Gothe and colleagues assigned 118 older adults to eight weeks of either hatha yoga or stretching-strengthening exercise. Only the yoga group improved significantly in attention and processing speed — measured with objective neuropsychological tests. A follow-up analysis found the same for executive function.

  • A systematic review of randomized controlled trials in healthy older adults concluded that yoga-based interventions consistently improve attention, processing speed, memory, and executive function compared with active controls (Complementary Therapies in Medicine).

  • Reviews of slow pranayamic breathing document a reliable physiological signature: enhanced vagal tone, calmer autonomic balance, improved mood, cognition and stress perception (Explore: The Journal of Science and Healing, 2025; multiple RCTs).

Notice what this research is measuring: not relaxation as an escape, but attention as trainable infrastructure. Eight weeks of postures and breath measurably rebuilt the exact faculties the digital economy erodes.

And there is a subtler finding hiding in Sadhguru's own teaching. He observes that a dedicated yogic practitioner's resting heart rate routinely drops into the thirties and forties — the body running at supreme efficiency, highly active yet deeply restful even amid demanding situations. Modern cardiology would call it exceptional autonomic balance. Yoga calls it Tuesday morning.

That matters, because the answer to the digital age is not amputation. The PNAS study proved blocking helps; but you cannot block your way through a world built on connectivity. The durable solution is upgrading the operator, not just limiting the machine — and classical hatha yoga is precisely the upgrade protocol for the operator's hardware.

An Operating System for Consciousness

Yoga, in Sadhguru's framing, is not exercise or religion — it is a user manual for the most sophisticated device you own.

Classical hatha yoga has a precise engineering logic. Literally: ha means sun, tha means moon. Hatha yoga is the science of bringing balance between the solar and lunar dimensions within your own system — two energies that modern life throws wildly out of phase, one screen-scroll at a time.

It goes further. All physical creation, Sadhguru teaches, is a perfection of geometry. The human body is itself a complex geometric structure, and hatha yoga is the practice of aligning your individual geometry with the larger cosmic geometry. When the system comes into alignment, it stops leaking energy into friction — and becomes what he calls an antenna capable of perceiving far more than any phone ever will.

The mechanism by which this restores attention sounds almost like software architecture: create space between you and your instruments.

If you consciously get your body into different postures, you can elevate your consciousness.

Posture and consciousness run in both directions. Every emotion you have ever felt had a physical shape — anger, grief, joy each hold the body differently. Classical yoga uses that connection deliberately: eighty-four classical postures, each a precise configuration through which the body stops generating distraction and starts generating stillness.

All stress lives in the collapse of inner space — the moment you are your anxiety rather than someone who has anxiety. A settled posture restores the gap. Thoughts still arise; notifications still arrive; but you are no longer fused with either. You become the stable desktop on which windows open and close, instead of the window that keeps getting opened.

And unlike every app promising focus, this one compounds. Mental alertness, Sadhguru notes, is merely a survival instrument — a dog has better alertness than you. Awareness is different: it is an instrument for creation. Alertness helps you react; awareness lets you choose. That difference is the entire game of being human in an automated age.

There is even a direct answer to the sedentary crisis of the digital age. Modern man barely uses his body, and the unspent energy doesn't disappear — it turns inward:

The whole world itself is going through a unique kind of neurosis... simply because modern man has stopped using his body to a large extent.

This is why hatha yoga — actual physical discipline, not just sitting meditation — is the right medicine for a generation that sits all day and wonders why its mind won't sit still. The body's compulsive energy gets worked out consciously, the nervous system regains balance, and the mind settles because the body finally lets it.

Even the Tools Can Serve

None of this requires hating technology — or retreating from it. Yoga has never been anti-tool; yogis have always been the most advanced technologists of their era, working with instruments far subtler than silicon.

Sadhguru notes that even what people call the occult was simply technology — subjective technology. Masters once spent years learning to transmit a single instruction across a continent; today you exchange phone numbers. Outer tech caught up with inner tech. The question was never which tool — it was always who holds it, and from what depth.

The lesson is not rejection but relationship: use the tool consciously, or it uses you compulsively. A phone in the hand of an aligned system is a communication device. The same phone in the hand of an unaligned system is a slot machine that pays in dopamine.

Where to Begin: Classical Hatha Yoga

This is not an argument for another app, another hack, another productivity ritual. It is an argument for the original technology — the body itself, worked deliberately through the classical practices as they are taught:

  • Your daily asana practice — the classical postures as transmitted, held with attention, aligning the system's geometry. Research shows eight weeks of consistent practice measurably improves attention and processing speed.
  • Surya Kriya — the classical process that balances the solar and lunar dimensions of the system through precisely designed postures, breath, and attention. Where Surya Namaskar is preparation, Surya Kriya is the deeper practice — activating the solar energy within so the whole system runs steadier, warmer, and more alive.
  • Nadi Shuddhi — the classical purification of the nadis through alternate-nostril breathing. Slow, conscious breathwork demonstrably shifts the autonomic nervous system toward calm and sharpens cognition — and this practice has been doing it for centuries.
  • Attention itself as the practice — whatever posture or breath you do, do it with total attention. That is the difference between exercise and hatha yoga.

Then try one experiment this week: notice, five times a day, that you are about to pick up the phone — and take one conscious breath first. Not to resist the phone. To remember who is picking it up.

That gap — half a second of awareness before the reflex — is where the entire battle for the human future is won or lost. Machines are becoming brilliant at everything except being alive. The one advantage you were born with is trainable, measurable, and free.

The Upgrade That Matters

The digital age gave us six hours of daily glass and a low hum of ambient anxiety. The next age promises machines that think faster than we ever will.

Fine. Let them think.

Your work was never to compete with the machine at its own game. Your work is to do the one thing no machine can do: be fully present to existence, from a depth that has no algorithm. Everything else — the phones, the AI, the wonders and the worries — is downstream of whether the human holding the tools is awake.

We upgraded our tools for ten thousand years. The remaining upgrade is inward.

The most sophisticated technology in the known universe is reading this sentence.
